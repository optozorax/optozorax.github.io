#What this document and chat are about

This document systematizes interesting information on the following topics:

  • Evolution simulation
  • Artificial life
  • Neural networks
  • Cellular automata
  • Artificial intelligence

Goal: to expand your mind, get inspired and write your own simulation of life, of evolution, or of something whose study brings us closer to creating General/Strong AI. Or just to write one for educational purposes.

It is built on the same principle as the 4D (in Russian) and hyperbolic (in Russian) repositories: there is a chat where these topics are discussed and interesting materials are posted, and then they are systematized into a document like this on GitHub.

Chat: telegram:emergevolution (in Russian)

Everyone interested is invited to the chat to discuss these topics, and to criticize and improve this document.

How you can improve it:

  • Suggest new material
  • Add comments and analysis to the existing material (for example, analyze an article, a video, a program). You can see that comments are lacking in many places here. You can take on this work if you're interested in figuring something out.

  • Focusing on the individual creature, so that it is as smart as possible

#What is Emergevolution?

#2Etymology

The word was coined by optozorax from two words: emergence and evolution. He first learned the word "emergence" from this video:
Emergence – How Stupid Things Become Smart Together Channel: Kurzgesagt – In a Nutshell
7:30

Wikipedia says: Emergence in systems theory is the appearance in a system of properties that its elements don't have on their own; the irreducibility of a system's properties to the sum of the properties of its components.

#2The problem

All evolution simulations known to the author have the property that they converge to some single solution and can't grow endlessly in complexity and in the diversity of their forms, the way it happens in real life and in nature on Earth.

And this is very sad, because even with huge computing power we can't just run some evolution simulation program, wait a couple of years and get very interesting results: no such programs are known to exist.

That's why the author is concerned with the problem of getting an evolution simulation that can grow potentially forever, constantly making its creatures more complex.

And it's this endlessly growing evolution that he called emergevolution.

He wants to create it at least in order to explore it and see what comes out; potentially it could help us understand our own evolution and the development of intelligence.

Below are examples of how different authors define it in their own way:

This term was coined long before the author of this document; there's even a Wikipedia page: Emergent evolution. Only they describe it not as a type of evolution, but as a hypothesis about the origin of the mind.

The PostNauka video says:

Even though artificial life has achieved such successes over this time, the main problem it faced still remains unsolved: the problem of how the complexity of organisms can increase in the course of evolution.

It's also called Open-Ended Evolution. You can probably search for scientific papers under this name and publish your own.

The evolvingai.org website defines this term as:

Creating computational evolutionary processes that--like natural evolution--endlessly produce a diversity of complex, interesting things.

At the 17th minute of the video about the Tierra program, its author says that he also wants to create an evolution that becomes endlessly more complex.

#4Snake problems

The problem with almost all implementations of snake evolution is that the snake can't remember where it has been before and what shape it has arranged its body into. That means it arranges its body either to get to the food as quickly as possible, or according to a single pattern. But it's unlikely to arrange its body sensibly enough once it has eaten too much food and its body takes up 90% of the board. You can see how a snake should ideally play here:
The Perfect Snake Game Channel: Omar Ghatasheh
2:27

A simple memoryless neural network can't win like that.

One way to solve this problem is to feed a much larger amount of state into the input, for example a larger area of the board. That way the network can learn the complex patterns it forms itself and how to deal with them. For example, this is how foo52ru did it.

Another way is to directly give the network the ability to remember, or to plan steps in its head, or to use Monte Carlo Tree Search, but nobody has done that yet.

#4Ideas for development

There are a couple of ideas for how this could be developed:

  • Give them the ability to sense the density of the particles around them, where the closer the particles, the stronger the density.
  • Similarly, a sense of the density of surrounding enemies
  • The density works over 360 degrees. You could dedicate 36 neurons to it, for example

And give them more time on the field, so that they manage to eat these particles and take into account where the enemies go, in order to collect as many as possible

And then test this on different fields:

  • like on that website, where the field has one center
  • where there are several centers
  • where everything is spread evenly

Or you could make two rings of density sensing at different distances, feed them to the creature, so that it learns to tell the distance to objects from them. Then we connect a separate neural network that moves in the given direction, visualize this direction and see at a high level what the neural network wants!

#2Derivative, gradient

The derivative of a function is an operation on a function that returns a new function; the new function shows how strongly the result of the function changes as a given variable changes. For more, see Wikipedia.

You can take the derivative only of a function that returns a real number (well, or a complex one, etc., but not a discrete one, not a natural number). You can also take the derivative with respect to different variables of a function, but only those that are real numbers. This is called a partial derivative.

Let's say we have a function: run_simulation(alpha, beta, genome) -> score. It shows how many points a creature will earn with the real-number parameters alpha and beta, and with genome, which is not a real number but a set of bits. genome defines some discrete structure of the creature, for example the number of layers and the number of neurons in each layer, all of it laid out in bits, say. The function returns a single real number: the creature's fitness score in this world. The bigger this number, the better.

Let's try to take the partial derivative of run_simulation with respect to the variable alpha; since it is a real number, we can do that. We get the function run_simulation_d_alpha(alpha, beta, genome) -> dscore. This function shows how strongly the score will change for different values of alpha with the parameters beta and genome fixed.

The gradient is almost the same as the derivative, except that it is a vector of derivatives with respect to all possible variables. That is, the new function returns not one number but several at once, one for each parameter: run_simulation_gradient(alpha, beta, genome) -> (d_alpha_score, d_beta_score). So we take the derivative with respect to all parameters at once.

The gradient is a vector that points in the direction in which the function increases.

I didn't go into details like partial derivatives, different coordinates and Jacobian matrices, just to keep things simple. I hope you'll learn all of this yourself when you study at university.

Gradients are used very heavily in tons of optimization methods (and so in neural networks), which is why I started with them.

#2Optimization methods

An optimization method is a method that finds the minimum/maximum of a given function that returns a single number. From here on we'll say that an optimization method always looks for a minimum, because you can find a maximum through a minimum by simply putting a minus in front of the function.

That is, this method takes a function that returns a single real number and finds the parameters for which this returned number is at its minimum.

The process of finding the minimum is called optimization.

An example with our function run_simulation: an optimization method will find the parameters at which the score reaches its maximum value. And the parameters of this function are the creature's genome. So with this method we'll find the creature that best solves the task we need in the simulation.

Usually all optimization methods work with functions that take real numbers and return real numbers. So finding the optimal genome in the run_simulation function won't really work (but there's a small "but", which I'll get to later).

Also, optimization methods very often require computing gradients, which is why I started talking about them. Gradient-free optimization methods exist too, but that's a separate topic, and I'll get to it later.

#3Gradient descent

This method works with a function of many variables. You've probably heard that neural networks are trained with exactly this method.

All this method needs is a starting point and a way to compute the gradient, and it will find the minimum of a smooth function.

Wikipedia has great illustrations.

Once again: gradient descent is not some component of neural networks, it's just an optimization method that happens to perform well. In fact, neural networks can be optimized with anything: a genetic algorithm, the multidimensional Newton's method, random search, anything that can find a minimum. But it's gradient descent that works best from a practical point of view.

#3These are all local search methods

There are two kinds of optimization methods: local and global. A local method finds different minima depending on the starting point and on luck. It won't necessarily find the smallest minimum, because it simply has no information about the other hills, since it's very limited in what it computes.

A global optimization method can find all minima and choose the smallest among them. But in the general case this is exotic, so don't hope that someone will ever invent a global optimization method that is usable in practice.

Almost all optimization methods aim at finding local minima. If you want to find all possible minima and invent a global optimization method, then you'd better not open this can of worms. First, because local minima should usually be enough, and second, because you can spend way too much effort on finding all the minima and get only, say, a 10% improvement over a local one.

#2What is a neural network

Some people have this stereotype that modern neural networks are successful precisely because they take the ideas for their structure from the brains of living beings. Not really. Read, for example, the article about the perceptron, which is the basis of all modern neural networks. Does this even hypothetically resemble what happens in the brain? Of course not: there are no loops, no memory elements, it's just... something very linear.

In fact, mathematically the perceptron boils down to multiplying a matrix by a vector and applying an activation function to the vector. So neural networks are more about matrices than about separate functioning elements called neurons.

If you don't know what matrices are, just think of multiplying a matrix by a vector as a way to write down many multiplications and additions done in parallel.

And a neural network lets you easily write down an approximation of some function using numbers. For example, you have a complicated function sin(x * ln(cos(x) + 2)), and you want to approximate it on the interval [-1, 1], knowing only its values. Well, with a neural network you're guaranteed to find some numbers that approximate this function with an error of 0.0001 (or any number you like). So a neural network is a way to define a function through numbers.

Then what is a neural network that takes a picture and returns who is in it, a dog or a cat? It's an approximation of a real function (an oracle) that always correctly tells what is a dog and what is a cat. That is, we don't know the inner workings of the oracle function (and never will), but we can approximate it just by taking its inputs and outputs.

On the other hand, you can think of it this way: we don't approximate some nonexistent function from its inputs and outputs, we simply minimize a loss function, and thanks to the large number of degrees of freedom of a neural network we do it well. In the end the neural network works well enough for practical purposes.

Actually, there are a million other ways to define functions through numbers, but matrix multiplication seems the simplest and the most optimal.

One more important thought: the current success of neural networks, again, isn't a success of neural networks themselves and of taking their structure from the brains of living beings; it's a success of optimization methods and of automatic differentiation methods, which I'll talk about later.

Just look at these matrix multiplications and at finding the minimum of a function by taking the gradient. Where is the biology here? Where are the processes that actually happen in the brains of living beings? They aren't here: machine learning and "neural networks" have long been a field far removed from biology.

And in general, I think we should stop calling all this "neural networks" and explaining that a neural network consists of neurons that send signals onward, because this kind of popularization puts the emphasis in the wrong place and confuses the average person.

#2Computing the derivative and the gradient numerically

At school you learned how to take the derivative of a function whose analytic expression you know, for example: f(x) = sin(x) * cos(x^2), and f'(x) = cos(x) * cos(x^2) - 2 * x * sin(x) * sin(x^2).

But how do you take the derivative of a function whose expression you don't know, or which can't be written down at all? The numerical derivative! It's computed by definition:

f'(x) ≈ (f(x+0.00001) - f(x))/0.00001

Where 0.00001 can be replaced by any other number, depending on your needs.

So to approximately compute the derivative, you need to compute the function itself 2 times: at the original point and at a point shifted by a constant.

For our function run_simulation, this means we have to run the simulation twice!

Okay, and what if we want to compute the gradient? Well, the same thing, only for each variable. So if we want to numerically compute the gradient of a function with N parameters, we'll have to compute the function N+1 times with different parameters.

Smell that burning CPU? Now imagine we have a neural network with 400 parameters, you use this network to tell whether a picture shows a cat or a dog, and the network is being optimized to tell them apart better. Then to get the gradient we'd have to run the network on each picture 401 times! That's very bad.

#2Backpropagation for computing the gradient

Actually, just look at Wikipedia.

It's a method that lets you compute the gradients of a neural network with respect to all variables while evaluating the network only once! Sure, the method is complicated and you have to record a lot of information, but the network is still evaluated just 1 TIME!

It's precisely thanks to the invention of this method that neural networks took off and became a promising field at all. Because nobody is going to compute the gradients of a neural network of size N by evaluating the network N times.

#2Computational graph

So we've learned that for an evaluated neural network the gradients can be computed in O(1) using backpropagation.

But actually things are much better. We can do the same for any linear (and even nonlinear) computation.

To compute some formula, you can build a computational graph in advance, where traversing it in one direction in a special way gives us the values, and traversing it in the other direction in another way gives us the gradient. I won't explain how it works, just know that it works like that. For the details, see pages 69-81 of Nikolenko's book "Deep Learning" (in Russian).

In general, the process of building a computational graph and then taking derivatives through it is called automatic differentiation.

And you can compute gradients with "backpropagation" not only for neural networks but for almost anything, including simulations. For example, here's differentiable physics.

#2Noise

Most of the optimization methods here are designed for functions without noise. Gradient descent, for example. It will work well only on a smooth function, where you can always clearly see which way the slope goes. And if your function is very noisy, gradient descent will just stupidly walk in a random direction at every point and get nowhere.

To get rid of noise, people usually use all sorts of regularization, or switch to an optimization method that is designed to handle noise.

In a field like training agents, noise is the norm. So when you choose an optimization method, pay attention to this, or run experiments.

#2Other optimization methods

There are a hundred or so other methods for finding the minimum of a function. Here I'll describe the most practically useful ones, and also just so that you know they exist.

#3Newton's method

This method requires computing the Hessian matrix, which consists of second partial derivatives and has size NxN. So if your neural network has 1 000 000 parameters, the matrix has to be of size 10^12. Which is a bit much. And computing all of it takes a very long time.

But on the upside, it converges in fewer iterations than gradient descent, and it finds the direction to move in much more stably.

For small problems you can quite well use it, if you're too lazy to figure out why gradient descent doesn't converge.

#3Quasi-Newton methods

These methods let you avoid storing and computing the Hessian matrix; instead they use an approximation of it, sometimes storing it, sometimes not. To do this, they use only information about the gradients.

An example of such a method: BFGS.

If you don't want to deal with tuning gradient descent, you'd better use BFGS; Newton's method, who needs it.

#3Bayesian optimization

This is a global search method that tries to use the minimum number of function evaluations to get the maximum of information.

Just look at the GIF of how this method works in this repository. All the other information is also there, and I see no point in retelling it.

#3CMA-ES

A very promising algorithm for optimizing problems where we don't know the gradient and there's a lot of noise. It works on parameters that are real numbers.

The paper.

A page with tutorials and source code in different languages.
CMA-ES ΓÇô a Stochastic Second-Order Method for Function-Value FreeNumerical Optimization Channel: Microsoft Research
56:17

Slides from the video above.

From the slides, you can tell that the algorithm is based on a multivariate normal distribution and, depending on the information it gets, adjusts this distribution so that its ellipse is oriented and shifted towards the region with the highest probability of success. Plus a bunch of other tricks; in theory it should work very well.

This method is very popular, I recommend it.

#3Genetic algorithm

Remember that all the optimization methods above work only with real numbers, and none of them allows for integers or any kind of discrete structure. Well, this method is a total game changer, because it can work with exactly that: discrete structures (remember genome in the run_simulation function).

It's based on evolution and mutations. It has already been explained a million times in the videos earlier in this document, so I won't repeat it.

Let's take the definition from Wikipedia: A genetic algorithm is a heuristic search algorithm used to solve optimization and modeling problems by randomly selecting, combining and varying the sought parameters, using mechanisms similar to natural selection in nature.

An important part of a genetic algorithm is crossover. So when you don't use crossover, formally you can't say that you're using a "genetic algorithm"; formally you're just using an "evolutionary algorithm". But since nobody cares, everyone says "genetic algorithm".

So if you want to search for the optimal program or the optimal body shape, this method may come in handy. I don't know any other methods for optimizing discrete parameters. If you know of something like that, please, please tell us in the chat.

Unfortunately, nobody has come up with as huge a science for discrete structures as the one for real numbers: no gradients for you, no line searches. That's probably why everyone strives to turn any discrete structure into a real-valued one. For example, you can represent an arbitrary neural network as a graph (a discrete structure) and try to optimize its structure, or you can represent the neural network as a matrix whose structure is defined entirely by real numbers. Obviously, the second approach will be more successful at solving the optimization problem, because the math there is just plain better.

#3When crossover is justified and when it isn't

Whether to use crossover in evolution is a tricky topic: on the one hand it can bring a negative result, on the other a positive one. To figure it out, we can look at a couple of examples:

A Twitter thread about crossover having a negative effect on a genetic algorithm solving the traveling salesman problem.

A striking example of when crossover comes in handy is Onigiri's video (in Russian). Here crossover is applied to the weights of neural networks that are roughly the same. Crossover here can be explained like this: it lets you find a point in the middle between two good close points. That is, in this case crossover would have performed very badly if the networks differed too much in their weights: such offspring would simply be unviable.

And this thought is confirmed above too.

As for crossover of discrete structures, it's a very controversial topic, and it has to be considered separately for each specific case.

If you're making your own genetic algorithm over discrete structures, better not do crossover.

Crossover is the kind of thing you should only use when you clearly understand what you're doing. In other cases ordinary mutations are enough.

#3Natural selection

This method is used in artificial life, but it's not really an optimization method.

This term came up somewhere in the cyberbiologists' chat. It means the following: we create some environment and put creatures into it that can reproduce and die, and even interact with and affect each other. In that case you don't even need to program any optimization algorithm specifically; you only need to define what lets creatures reproduce and what makes them die, and add mutations during reproduction; and they will evolve by themselves. Different generations of creatures will exist at different times, no restrictions are imposed here.

In a genetic algorithm it's assumed that all creatures of a given generation are born and die at the same time, and are evaluated in the environment independently of each other. On top of that, there's a special function that says how well adapted a given creature is. In natural selection there's nothing like that, and everything is determined by the environment, implicitly.

With this method we make a kind of soup of simulated creatures that do something. It can lead either to something unusual (as in the case of foo52ru), or to no result at all (as in the case of Polyworld).

But this method obviously requires a large number of creatures on the field at the same time, and preferably a very, very large field, so that ecosystems can form.

#2Von Neumann automaton

A wiki link.

×1
gif

According to the PostNauka video, in this automaton you can encode an object that can create a copy of itself; it has to consist of several elements:

  • A set of instructions
  • A device that can copy the set of instructions
  • A device that can reproduce the automaton itself from this set of instructions

Interestingly, the structure of DNA and how cells use it were discovered only later!

But of course, such an automaton can't be subjected to evolution that easily, because changing 1 bit in the instructions can break the whole automaton; plus, the environment itself has no factors that would force it to change its "DNA". This is called the digital sterility problem.

#Writing your own simulation

#2Things to keep in mind

If you're writing a Snake game, you can keep the snake problems in mind.

#3Simulate and draw separately

Very many simulations are made so that the simulation runs in real time, with every step shown on the screen. This is bad, because the CPU can usually run the simulation much faster, but it's limited to 60 steps per second. You need to plan the application's architecture from the start so that the simulation is completely decoupled from the display. That way the simulation can be sped up, or turned into a separate console program.

For visualization, at the first stage you can draw the world into a picture or a GIF; for that you can just add libraries for the corresponding formats. Moreover, if you write the code for drawing the world into a picture right away, you can later adapt it for a GUI, while the other way around won't be as easy.

#3Defining a creature's behavior with a program

In the foo52ru simulation and some others, the behavior of a creature is defined by code. The problem with code is that the slightest change to it changes its result drastically. This is very bad for evolution, because evolution works well with things where small changes give a small result, so that it can feel its way towards something.

From this point of view, neural networks are very good, because a small change to their numbers changes the result only slightly, so you can feel your way towards the right direction of development.

The fact that it worked for foo52ru is more likely just luck; besides, his task is simple enough to be solved by brute-forcing code. If you solve a harder task, creatures driven by code will never evolve into anything decent.

So if you want to make your creatures controlled by code, think twice, or try to come up with code where small changes to the code make small changes to the result.

So far there's this idea (in Russian):

We have the original algorithm; let's denote it as a function:

func(input) -> output

We make a mutation; the algorithm can change very strongly and becomes:

funcMutated(input) -> output

And then, in order not to introduce this very strong mutation into the algorithm, we do this:

funcResult(input) = func(input) * 0.9 + funcMutated(input) * 0.1 -> output

So in essence, the mutation has a strong effect, but we dampen its influence and gradually increase its weight.

You can have many such weighted mutations at the same time.

The main problem with this idea is that the outputs must be real numbers. If they are discrete, you can't apply this. Although it can work if the function gives the probability of ending up in the right place; then weights fit right in, we just modify the weights. Then again, if these are real numbers anyway, maybe neural networks are better after all?

#3Training neural networks in blocks

An idea from my article:

An example based on training a "human": if we need a 3D human to learn to walk on two legs, we know that he walks without falling, so first we need to train some block: keeping balance. We can put this human in an upright position with the legs shifted to the side, with some velocity, and make him either keep his balance or get into a position where the balance is kept.

And then, once we've trained this balance module, combine it with the walking module and train it so that he learns to move his legs forward, somehow keeps his balance, and doesn't fall while doing it. And the balance block can be combined in parallel with the jumping block, etc., so that he learns to keep his balance in completely different situations.

When he learns to walk, this will help him by keeping him from falling, and the walking block won't have to do the work of keeping balance.
